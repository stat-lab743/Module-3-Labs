THE STUDY

Which training method works better?

A research team studies whether training method affects performance on a standardized task. Thirty participants are randomly assigned to one of two conditions, with 15 participants in each group. Each person receives one method and completes the task once.

Standard Training

Instruction followed by independent practice.

Interactive Training

Instruction, guided practice, and immediate feedback.

After training, everyone completes the same task. Performance scores range from 0–100; higher scores indicate better performance. This is a synthetic dataset designed for learning.

Do participants receiving Interactive Training perform differently from participants receiving Standard Training?

Conceptual help: hypotheses

Hypotheses describe population means (μ), not the observed sample means. A null hypothesis represents no population mean difference. An alternative can ask about a difference in either direction or specify a direction in advance. Which matches “perform differently”?

jamovi Help: common starting problems
  • CSV opens in a spreadsheet app? Launch jamovi first and open the saved file from its menu.
  • Score unavailable as a dependent variable? Check that its data type is numeric (Integer or Decimal) and its measure type is Continuous.
  • Grouping variable will not work? Set Training Method to Nominal and verify exactly two levels, with no extra spaces or categories.
  • Missing CI or effect size? Expand Additional Statistics; enable Mean difference and its 95% confidence interval, plus Effect size.
  • Output differs in sign? Check the group order before assuming the calculation is wrong.
Assumptions, briefly

Student’s test assumes independent observations, approximately normal scores within each population, and equal population variances. Look for severe outliers and markedly different spreads; independence comes from the design. Use Student’s for this teaching analysis. Welch’s test relaxes the equal-variance assumption and may give a different df and p; label it separately if explored. An assumption-test p value alone does not prove an assumption.

Make the decision

  1. Reject or fail to reject H0? Compare your p value with α and cite t, df, and p as evidence.
  2. Answer the research question in context. Describe the direction of the observed difference and which training method had the higher sample mean.
  3. Compare your finding with your Part 2 prediction. What did the analysis clarify?
Why does wording matter?

If p < .05, reject H0; otherwise fail to reject it. “Fail to reject” does not establish that population means are equal. A p value is not the probability that H0 is true, and statistical significance does not describe the size of a benefit.

PART 07 · WORD REPORT

Beyond statistical significance

A statistical result is evidence for a decision—not the entire decision.

  1. Interpret your 95% CI for the population mean difference in score points, stating the subtraction order. Does it include zero? How does that relate to your two-tailed test?
  2. Interpret Cohen’s d as a standardized mean difference. What does it add beyond the p value? If you use size labels, explain their limitations in this context.
  3. Critique the claim below. Use your observed difference, CI, and effect size, and identify at least two additional pieces of information needed before adopting a training method.
“Interactive training is statistically significant, so we should immediately replace Standard Training throughout the organization.”
Why does this matter?

Statistical significance concerns evidence against a null model. Practical significance concerns whether a difference is useful. Consider what a score-point gain means, training costs and time, uncertainty, and whether these participants and this task represent the intended setting. Use those considerations to construct your own argument.

CI and effect-size reminders

A 95% confidence procedure captures the true population difference in 95% of repeated samples under its assumptions; the interval is not the range containing 95% of individual scores. Cohen’s d divides the signed mean difference by a pooled within-group SD. Rough benchmarks of |d| ≈ 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 are sometimes called small, medium, and large, but context determines importance.

How the simulation calculates the test

Difference = MI − MS
SE = s × √(2/n)
t = difference / SE
df = 2n − 2

Here s is the shared within-group sample SD and n is the sample size in each group. The df is exact for this equal-n Student setup. The two-tailed p value is computed numerically from the Student t distribution, not a normal approximation. Only the summaries change; no random sample is generated.
