THE STUDY

Change is measurable. Its cause needs evidence.

A research team evaluates a brief attention-management intervention. Twenty-four participants complete a standardized performance task before a one-week program of focused-work practice and distraction-management strategies. They complete the same task again after the program. Scores range from 0–100; higher scores indicate better performance.

Every participant receives the intervention. There is no untreated comparison group and no random assignment to intervention versus control. Each row contains one person’s before and after scores. The dataset is synthetic and designed for learning.

Do performance scores differ, on average, from before to after the intervention?

Understand the study

One group, measured twice. Preserve the pairing.

  1. Identify the measured outcome and its measurement scale. Identify the within-person factor and its two levels.
  2. How many participants and how many observations are there? Explain why the observations are not all independent.
  3. Why does this study require a paired-samples t test rather than the independent-samples test used in Lab 6?
  4. What does one row represent? Why must each person’s before and after scores stay together?
Why does pairing matter?

The analysis focuses on each person’s change. Before and after values from the same person are related; changes from different participants are assumed independent. Sorting only one score column would break the pairing and corrupt the analysis.

jamovi Help

If a score is unavailable, set its data type to Integer or Decimal and measurement type to Continuous. If you see a grouping-variable box, switch to the paired-samples analysis. If signs are reversed, check pair order: reversing it changes the signs of the mean change, t, and dᶻ and changes CI [L, U] to [−U, −L]; the two-tailed p is unchanged.

Assumptions for paired data

The differences should be approximately normally distributed without severe outliers, and participants’ differences should be independent of each other. Inspect a Q–Q plot of differences if requested. Equal before/after variances are not required. The intended dependence within each pair is handled by analyzing changes.

PART 06 · WORD REPORT

Make the decision

Use α = .05 and the two-tailed paired test.

  1. At α = .05, reject or fail to reject H₀? Compare p with α and cite t, df, and p.
  2. Answer the research question in context. State the direction and size of the observed mean change.
  3. How does your result compare with your original prediction? Does a mean increase imply that every participant improved?
Careful decision language

If p < .05, reject H₀; otherwise fail to reject it. Failing to reject does not prove no change. The p value is not the probability that H₀ is true.

PART 07 · WORD REPORT

Did the intervention cause the change?

Statistical evidence and causal evidence answer different questions.

“Scores improved significantly, so the intervention caused the improvement and should be adopted immediately.”
  1. Interpret the 95% CI for population mean change in score points. Does it include zero, and how does this relate to your test?
  2. Interpret Cohen’s dᶻ. Explain what it adds beyond p and why it is based on variation in individual changes.
  3. Critique the claim below using your mean change, CI, and effect size. Explain why statistical significance alone does not prove practical importance or causation.
  4. Identify two plausible alternative explanations for change and propose a stronger study design. What additional information would help decide whether to adopt the intervention?
Why does this matter?

This design has no untreated comparison group and no random assignment to intervention versus control. Practice on the task, events during the week, or other changes could contribute. A randomized comparison group would help separate intervention effects from changes that might occur anyway. Consider costs, benefit size, uncertainty, and who the participants represent.

Effect size and confidence intervals

dᶻ = mean change / SD of individual changes. Its magnitude describes standardized change, not proof of causation. A 95% CI concerns population mean change, not the range containing 95% of individual changes. Context matters more than a fixed effect-size label.

How the paired test works

SE = sΔ / √n
t = mean change / SE
df = n − 1
dᶻ = mean change / sΔ

The p value is calculated numerically from the Student t distribution. These summaries describe complete paired observations; n is not the total number of scores.

  3. Reset, then move mean change toward zero. Hold SD and n fixed. Record a setting and explain what happens to the evidence.
